Compostable vs Biodegradable: What Do the Labels Really Mean?

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-11-25 · Category: Zero Waste Living

Walk down any supermarket aisle and you will see the words compostable and biodegradable on bags, cups, wipes and packaging. They sound reassuring, and they are often used as if they mean the same thing — but they do not. A product that is biodegradable may take years to break down, while a compostable product is designed to break down in a specific setting within a set time. Knowing the difference helps you choose better products and, just as importantly, put them in the right bin.

What Does Biodegradable Mean?

Biodegradable means that something can be broken down by living organisms, such as bacteria and fungi, into natural substances like water, carbon dioxide and biomass. The catch is that the label gives no time limit. A plastic bag labelled biodegradable might break down in a few months under the right conditions — or it might take years in a landfill, where there is little oxygen or moisture to help the process along. Some biodegradable plastics only break down in industrial facilities with high heat, not in a garden compost heap or in the sea. The word tells you that breakdown is possible, not that it will happen quickly or anywhere.

What Does Compostable Mean?

Compostable is a stricter, more useful claim. A compostable product is designed to break down completely into compost — a nutrient-rich material that can feed soil — within a set time and under specific conditions. There are two main kinds. Home compostable products break down in an ordinary garden compost heap within about six months. Industrially compostable products need the higher temperatures of a commercial composting facility, and they will not break down properly in a home heap. Look for a clear certification mark, such as the seedling logo in Europe or BPI certification in North America, which tells you the claim has been tested.

Compostable vs Biodegradable at a Glance

The easiest way to remember the difference is to think of it as a promise. Biodegradable promises that something will eventually break down, with no promise about when or where. Compostable promises that something will break down into useful compost, within a set time, in a named setting. That makes compostable the stronger claim — but only if you can actually get the item to the right composting route. A compostable coffee cup thrown in a landfill will behave much like ordinary rubbish, because landfills are not designed for composting. And a compostable bag put in the recycling bin can contaminate a whole load of recycling, because it is not the same material as ordinary plastic.

Why the Labels Can Mislead

These words are loosely regulated in many places,. A bag can be called biodegradable even if it takes years to disappear. Some products are labelled compostable but only break down in industrial facilities that many areas do not have. Others contain a mix of plant-based and ordinary plastic, so only part of the product breaks down. The safest habit is to read past the front of the pack: look for a certification mark, check whether it says home or industrial compostable, and look for the disposal instructions. If the pack does not say how to dispose of it, the claim is probably not worth much.

How to Dispose of Each Properly

Getting disposal right matters more than choosing the perfect product. Home compostable items can go in your garden compost heap along with fruit peels and garden waste — our guide to starting composting at home explains how to build a heap that works. Industrially compostable items belong in a food waste or compost collection if your area offers one; if it does not, they should go in general waste, because they will not compost in a landfill. Biodegradable plastics should not go in the recycling bin unless the packaging clearly says they can — they are a different material from ordinary plastic and can spoil a recycling load. When in doubt, check your local council's waste guide, which lists exactly what each bin accepts.

Simple Habits That Cut the Confusion

The simplest way to deal with confusing labels is to need them less. Carry a reusable bag instead of choosing between compostable and plastic ones. Use a refillable bottle and a reusable coffee cup instead of single-use versions of either kind. Buy loose fruit and vegetables where you can, and choose products with minimal packaging in the first place. When you do need a labelled product, pick one with a clear certification and a clear disposal route you can actually use.
