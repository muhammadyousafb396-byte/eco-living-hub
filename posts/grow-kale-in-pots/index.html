Grow Kale in Pots: A Beginner Guide to Fresh Homegrown Kale

Kale is one of the easiest and most rewarding vegetables you can grow in a pot. It is tough, forgiving, and keeps producing leaves for months from a single plant. A few pots on a sunny balcony or patio can give you fresh greens for salads, soups and smoothies from spring right through to the first frosts.

Why Kale Loves Pots

Kale does not need a vegetable bed. It grows happily in a container, and pots actually solve several common problems. The soil in a pot warms up faster in spring, drains well, and is easy to keep free of weeds. You can keep it close to the kitchen door so picking a few leaves for dinner takes seconds. Kale is also a cold-hardy plant, so a pot on a sheltered balcony will keep going long after tender summer crops have finished.

Choose the Right Variety

There are three main types of kale, and all of them grow well in pots. Curly kale is the classic frilly-leaved kind you see in shops; it is hardy and productive. Lacinato kale, also called dinosaur or Tuscan kale, has long dark blue-green leaves and a sweeter, milder flavour. Red Russian kale has flat, purple-tinged leaves and is one of the most tender varieties when picked young. If you are short on space, look for a dwarf variety, which stays smaller and suits a 30-centimetre pot.

What You Need

Each kale plant needs a pot at least 30 centimetres wide and deep, with drainage holes in the bottom. Fill it with good multi-purpose compost, and mix in a handful of garden compost if you have it. Kale is a hungry plant, so rich soil makes a real difference to the size of the leaves. Stand the pot where it gets at least six hours of sun a day, although kale will still grow in light shade — it will just grow more slowly.

How to Sow and Plant

You can sow kale seeds directly into the pot from mid-spring, or start them indoors a few weeks earlier and move the seedlings out once they have four true leaves. Sow seeds about one centimetre deep, water gently, and keep the compost damp until they sprout, which usually takes one to two weeks. Thin the seedlings to one strong plant per pot. If you buy young plants from a nursery, plant them at the same depth they were growing before, firm the compost around them, and water well.

Caring for Potted Kale

Water is the main job. Pots dry out much faster than garden soil, so check the compost every day in warm weather and water when the top few centimetres feel dry. A deep water less often is better than a light sprinkle every day, because it encourages the roots to grow down. Feed the plants every two weeks with a liquid seaweed feed once they are growing strongly. Remove any yellowing leaves from the bottom of the plant, as this keeps air moving and reduces the chance of disease.

Harvesting Leaves for Months

The best thing about kale is that you pick it leaf by leaf, and the plant keeps growing. Start harvesting when the plant has about eight leaves and the outer ones are the size of your hand. Pick from the outside first, leaving the small leaves in the centre to keep growing. Take only a few leaves from each plant at a time, and never strip a young plant bare. With regular picking, one plant can keep you in fresh leaves for months. Young leaves are tender enough for salads, while larger leaves are best lightly steamed or stirred into soups and stews.

Common Problems

Kale has few enemies, but a few are worth watching for. Cabbage white butterflies lay eggs on the leaves, and the green caterpillars can strip a plant quickly, so check the undersides of leaves every few days and pick off any caterpillars you find. A fine net over the pot is the simplest protection. Slugs love young kale, so raise pots off the ground on feet or bricks and check after rain. In hot weather kale can bolt, which means it runs to flower and the leaves turn bitter — keeping the compost moist helps.

A Small Pot, a Long Harvest

Growing kale in pots is proof that you do not need a garden to grow your own food. One pot, a sunny spot and a little regular watering can give you months of fresh, healthy greens. Start with a single plant this season, learn what it likes, and add a second variety next time. For more small-space ideas, try growing spinach in pots or growing herbs on a windowsill alongside your pots.
