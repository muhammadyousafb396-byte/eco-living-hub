Save Seeds From Your Garden: A Simple Beginner Guide

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-11-01 · Category: Urban Gardening

  • Open-pollinated and heirloom seeds vs hybrids
  • When and how to collect seeds
  • Wet seeds: fermenting tomato seeds
  • Drying and storing seeds properly
  • The bottom line

Every tomato, bean and flower in your garden holds the start of next year's plants. Saving seeds means collecting them at the end of the season, drying them properly, and storing them until spring. It costs nothing, keeps your favourite varieties going year after year, and is one of the most satisfying parts of gardening. Start with one or two easy plants, follow the steps below, and you will have a small envelope of seeds ready to sow when the weather warms up.

Which plants are easiest for beginners

Beans, peas, lettuce, tomatoes and marigolds are all forgiving choices for a first try. Beans and peas are self-pollinating, so their seeds usually grow true to the parent plant. Lettuce produces masses of tiny seeds from one plant allowed to flower. Tomatoes need one extra step, fermenting, explained below, but it is simple once you have done it once. Avoid starting with plants that cross-pollinate easily, such as squash, cucumbers, sweetcorn and brassicas like cabbage. Their flowers mix pollen between varieties, so saved seed may grow into something different from the parent. As a rule, save seed only from healthy plants that grew well in your conditions, never from weak or diseased ones.

Open-pollinated and heirloom seeds vs hybrids

One idea matters more than any other in seed saving: whether a variety is open-pollinated or a hybrid. Open-pollinated and heirloom varieties have been grown and selected over many generations, and their seeds grow into plants that look and taste like the parent. These are the best seeds to save. Hybrid varieties, often labelled F1 on the seed packet, are crosses between two parent lines. Their seeds will still grow, but the plants may differ from the parent in size, flavour or yield. If you are not sure what you grew, check the original seed packet or plant label. For sowing saved seed next spring, our guide to seed starting indoors walks through trays, soil and timing for strong seedlings.

When and how to collect seeds

Seeds must be fully ripe before you collect them, because unripe seeds often fail to germinate. For dry seeds such as beans and peas, leave the pods on the plant until they are dry, papery and rattling. Then pick them on a dry day, shell them into a bowl, and spread the seeds out to dry further indoors. For lettuce, let one plant flower, then shake dry seed heads into a paper bag. For flowers, wait until the heads are brown and dry, then rub them gently over a bowl to release the seeds. Wet seeds, such as tomato seeds, sit inside juicy fruit. Pick a fully ripe fruit from a healthy plant, scoop the seeds and pulp into a jar, and add a little water.

Wet seeds: fermenting tomato seeds

Tomato seeds are coated in a jelly-like layer that stops them germinating inside the fruit. Fermenting removes that coating and helps kill some seed-borne diseases. Scoop the seeds and pulp from a ripe tomato into a small jar, add a splash of water, and cover the jar loosely. Leave it at room temperature for two to three days, stirring once a day. The mixture will smell slightly sour and a layer of mould may form on top. When most good seeds have sunk to the bottom, pour off the floating pulp, rinse the seeds in a sieve, and spread them on a plate or wax paper to dry. Never dry seeds on paper towels, because they stick and tear when you try to remove them.

Drying and storing seeds properly

Seeds must be completely dry before storage, or they will mould in the envelope. Spread them in a single layer in a cool, dry place out of direct sunlight for one to two weeks. Store dry seeds in labelled paper envelopes, not plastic bags, because paper lets remaining moisture escape. Write the plant name, variety and date on every envelope while you still remember. Then put the envelopes in a sealed jar or tin in a cool, dark, dry place, such as a cupboard or the fridge. Most vegetable seeds keep well for two to five years this way. Growing plants in a small space works just as well with saved seed as with bought seed, so a few envelopes can fill pots, raised beds and balcony containers. Kitchen scraps can also become homemade plant fertilizer that keeps soil healthy all season.

The bottom line

Seed saving is a small habit with a big payoff. One healthy tomato plant can give you enough seed for years of tomatoes, and one bean plant can fill a whole row next season. Start with beans or lettuce, dry seed thoroughly, and label everything. Within a season or two, saving seed will feel like a natural part of gardening.
