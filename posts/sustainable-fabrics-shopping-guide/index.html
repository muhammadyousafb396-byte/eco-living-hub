Sustainable Fabrics Explained: What to Look For When You Shop

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-10-30 · Category: Sustainable Fashion

On this page

  • Why fabric choice matters so much
  • Fabrics worth looking for
  • Fabrics and claims to question
  • Labels that actually help
  • A simple shopping checklist

Every garment starts as a raw material, and that material choice shapes most of its environmental impact. The fabric decides how much water and land were used, which chemicals were involved, and whether the garment will shed microplastics or break down at the end of its life. Shopping for sustainable fabrics is about knowing which materials are genuinely better, which claims deserve a second look, and which labels you can trust.

Why fabric choice matters so much

Fibre production is where fashion's footprint begins. Conventional cotton is thirsty and often grown with heavy pesticide use. Polyester is made from fossil fuels and sheds tiny plastic fibres every time it is washed. Viscose can come from responsibly managed forests or from ancient forests cleared for pulp, and the label rarely tells you which. Choosing a better fabric does not fix every problem in fashion, but it is the single decision you make before you even try something on, and it is fully in your control.

Fabrics worth looking for

Organic cotton

Grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers, organic cotton uses less irrigation water than conventional cotton in most regions and is gentler on farming communities. Look for GOTS certification, which covers both farming and processing. One caution: a heavy, over-dyed organic cotton garment is still resource-hungry, so buy for quality and longevity first.

Linen

Made from flax, linen needs little water, almost no pesticides, and nearly every part of the plant gets used. It is strong, breathable, and gets softer with washing, which makes it one of the best everyday fabrics for warm weather. Pure linen wrinkles; that is part of its character. A linen shirt that lasts ten years beats three thin cotton shirts that wear out in a season.

Hemp

Hemp grows fast, improves the soil it grows in, and needs very little water or pesticide. The resulting fabric is durable and naturally resistant to mold, which suits jackets, jeans, and workwear. Modern processing has made hemp far softer, and hemp-cotton blends are an easy entry point.

TENCEL lyocell

TENCEL is a brand name for lyocell, a fibre made from wood pulp in a closed-loop process that recovers almost all of the solvent used. The result is a soft, drapey fabric that works well for dresses, blouses, and underwear. Check the label says TENCEL or lyocell rather than generic rayon; the production process is what makes the difference.

Recycled wool and recycled cotton

Recycled fibres keep existing material in use and avoid new farming or oil extraction. Recycled wool is warm and long-lasting, while recycled cotton is usually blended with virgin cotton for strength. Both are good choices, especially for knitwear and outerwear you will wear for years.

Fabrics and claims to question

Conventional polyester is the biggest red flag: cheap, everywhere, and plastic-based. Recycled polyester is better than virgin, but it still sheds microfibres in the wash, so use a laundry bag designed to catch them. Generic viscose and rayon deserve scrutiny unless the brand names its pulp source and process. Bamboo is the classic greenwashing trap: bamboo fabric is usually bamboo viscose, made through the same chemical-heavy process as other rayon, and the bamboo in the field has little to do with the finished cloth. If a brand leans on the word bamboo without explaining the process, treat it as marketing, not evidence.

Labels that actually help

Three certifications do most of the work. GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard) covers organic fibre content plus environmental and social rules through processing. OEKO-TEX Standard 100 tests the finished fabric for harmful substances, which matters for anything worn against skin. GRS (Global Recycled Standard) verifies recycled content and tracks it through the supply chain. No label is perfect, but a garment carrying one has been checked by someone other than the brand selling it.

A simple shopping checklist

  • Read the fibre label before the price tag. Aim for one of the better fabrics above, ideally over 70 percent of the garment.
  • Prefer one main fibre over complex blends, which are harder to recycle.
  • Ask how it washes. Cold wash, no tumble dryer, and fewer washes all extend a garment's life.
  • Buy less, but better. A well-made linen shirt worn a hundred times beats five trendy tops worn twice.

Finally, remember that the most sustainable garment is often the one you already own. Learning to care for your clothes so they last longer multiplies the value of every good fabric choice you make. And if budget is the barrier, these tips for shopping sustainably on a budget show how to find better fabrics without spending more. Fabric knowledge turns shopping from guesswork into a skill, and that skill pays off with every purchase.
