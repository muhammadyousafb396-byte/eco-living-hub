Clothing Repair Basics: Simple Mending Skills That Save Clothes

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-10-29 · Category: Sustainable Fashion

  • Sew On a Button That Stays Put
  • Fix Seams, Hems, and Small Tears

Most clothes are thrown away for small, fixable reasons: a missing button, a split seam, a hem that has come down, or a tiny hole. Learning a few basic mending skills can keep favourite clothes in use for years, save money on replacements, and reduce the number of garments that end up as waste. You do not need to be an expert sewer. A needle, thread, and a little patience will handle most everyday repairs, and each repair gets easier with practice. Start with one simple fix, and build your confidence from there.

Build a Small Mending Kit

Choose Thread That Matches

Thread that closely matches the fabric colour will blend in, even if your stitches are not perfect. For dark clothes, use dark thread. For light clothes, use a thread one shade darker than the fabric, as lighter thread can stand out. Use a thread weight suited to the fabric. Fine thread suits shirts and light dresses, while stronger thread suits jeans, coats, and bags. If you are unsure, ask at a fabric shop and take the garment with you. Staff can usually suggest the right needle and thread in a minute.

Sew On a Button That Stays Put

A button repair is the best first project. Thread the needle, knot the end, and position the button over the original marks on the fabric. Sew up through one hole and down through the opposite hole several times, keeping the stitches neat and even. For a shirt button, leave a small thread shank, a little space between button and fabric, by wrapping thread around the stitches a few times before finishing. This lets the button sit comfortably in its buttonhole. Finish with a few small stitches on the back and a firm knot. Check the other buttons too. If one has come loose, its neighbours may need tightening, and fixing them now prevents a second repair later.

Fix Seams, Hems, and Small Tears

A split seam is usually easy to repair. Turn the garment inside out, line up the original seam, pin it in place, and sew along the original stitch line with small, even stitches. Use a backstitch, where each stitch overlaps the last, for strength on seams that take strain, such as underarms and trouser legs. For a hem that has come down, fold it to the original crease, pin it, and sew with a slip stitch that catches only a few threads of the outer fabric, so stitches barely show. Press the repair with an iron on a setting suited to the fabric, and the hem will look neat again.

  • Repair damage as soon as you notice it, before it spreads.
  • Turn garments inside out to see seams and stitch lines clearly.
  • Pin fabric before sewing, so layers do not slip.
  • Test your stitch on a hidden area first if you are unsure.
  • Press repairs flat with an iron for a tidy finish.

Know When to Ask a Professional

Some repairs are worth handing to a tailor or repair shop. Zips, lined coats, leather, delicate fabrics, and structured jackets often need specialist tools and skills. A tailor can also adjust fit, shorten trousers, take in a waist, or replace a lining, often for less than the cost of a new garment. Ask for a price before you commit, and compare it with the value of the item to you, not only its shop price. A well-loved coat or a perfectly fitting pair of trousers is often worth repairing, even if a cheaper replacement exists. If you enjoy second-hand shopping, mending skills also help you say yes to good finds. Our thrift-store shopping guide shows how to spot clothes with good bones that only need a button or a hem.

Make Repair a Habit, Not a Chore

Repair becomes easier when it is part of your routine. Keep a small mending pile, set aside twenty minutes once a week, and work through one or two items at a time. Put on a podcast or music, and treat it as quiet, useful time rather than a task to rush. Teach children simple repairs too. Sewing on a button or patching jeans gives them a practical skill and shows them that clothes can be fixed, not only replaced. Over time, you will buy differently as well. You will notice strong seams, spare buttons, and fabrics that mend well, and you will choose clothes that are made to last. That shift, from replacing to repairing, is at the heart of a more sustainable capsule wardrobe, where fewer, better-loved clothes do more work.
