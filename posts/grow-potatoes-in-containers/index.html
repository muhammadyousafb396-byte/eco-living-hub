Grow Potatoes in Containers: A Beginner Guide to a Big Homegrown Harvest

Potatoes are one of the easiest and most satisfying vegetables to grow, and you do not need a garden bed to do it. A large pot, a grow bag, or even a bucket on a balcony or patio can produce a surprisingly generous harvest. Homegrown potatoes taste better than store-bought ones, cost very little to grow, and need almost no special skill. If you can water a plant and add soil every few weeks, you can grow potatoes.

Why Grow Potatoes in Containers?

Container growing solves the biggest problems of growing potatoes in the ground. You control the soil, so you avoid heavy clay and pests that live in garden beds. Harvesting is much easier too: instead of digging through soil with a fork and accidentally spearing potatoes, you simply tip the container out. Containers also work anywhere with enough sun, which makes potatoes a great crop for balconies, patios, and small yards. If you already grow food in pots, potatoes fit right in alongside tomatoes in containers and beans in pots.

Step 1: Choose the Right Variety

Start with seed potatoes, which are small potatoes grown specifically for planting and certified disease-free. Do not plant potatoes from the supermarket, as they are often treated to stop sprouting and can carry disease. For containers, early varieties are a great choice because they mature in about ten to twelve weeks and stay compact. Good options include first early varieties such as Rocket or Swift, and second earlies such as Charlotte, which is known for its flavour.

Step 2: Pick a Container and Fill It With Good Soil

Your container needs to be at least 40 litres, which is roughly the size of a large bucket or a standard grow bag. Bigger is better: a 60-litre container can hold three to four seed potatoes and produce a much larger harvest. Whatever you use, it must have drainage holes, because potatoes rot in waterlogged soil. Fill the container with a mix of quality potting compost and garden compost. Leave the top third of the container empty for now, because you will add more soil as the plants grow.

Step 3: Chit and Plant Your Seed Potatoes

A few weeks before planting, place your seed potatoes in a light, cool spot with the end that has the most eyes facing up. This process, called chitting, encourages short, strong green shoots to form. When the shoots are about 2 centimetres long, the potatoes are ready to plant. Plant them in late spring, after the last frost, with the shoots facing up and about 15 centimetres of soil underneath them.

Step 4: Earth Up as the Plants Grow

This is the most important step in container potato growing. When the green shoots reach about 15 centimetres tall, add more soil or compost around the stems until only the top few leaves show. Repeat this every two to three weeks as the plants grow, until the container is nearly full. This process, called earthing up, gives the plant more stem to form potatoes along, which is how you get a bigger harvest from one container. It also keeps the growing potatoes covered, which matters because potatoes that see sunlight turn green and become inedible.

Step 5: Water and Feed Regularly

Containers dry out faster than garden beds, especially in warm weather, so check the soil every day in summer. Push a finger a few centimetres into the soil: if it feels dry, water deeply until it drains from the bottom. Consistent watering matters most once the plants flower, because that is when the potatoes are swelling underground. Uneven watering can cause cracked or hollow potatoes, so try to keep the soil evenly moist.

When and How to Harvest

Early varieties are usually ready about ten to twelve weeks after planting, once the plants flower and the leaves start to yellow. For the main harvest, wait until the foliage has died back, then stop watering and leave the potatoes in the soil for another week or two so their skins firm up. Then tip the whole container onto a sheet or into a wheelbarrow and pick out the potatoes by hand. Let them dry in a cool, dark place for a few hours before storing them in paper bags or a dark box.

The Bottom Line

Growing potatoes in containers is one of the best first projects for a new gardener. The setup is cheap, the work is simple, and the reward is a basket of fresh potatoes you grew yourself. Start with one or two containers this season, earth up as the plants grow, and keep the soil moist. By harvest time, you will be planning which varieties to try next year, and your balcony garden will feel like a real little farm.
