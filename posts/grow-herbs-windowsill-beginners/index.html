Grow Herbs on a Windowsill: A Beginner Guide

Fresh herbs are the easiest win in home growing. A pot of basil on a sunny windowsill can supply a kitchen for months, costs far less than buying cut bunches that wilt in the fridge, and tastes noticeably better. You need no garden and no special equipment — just a sunny window, a few pots, and ten minutes a week. This guide covers the simplest herbs to start with, the setup that works, and the habits that keep them alive.

Why Grow Herbs on a Windowsill?

Herbs are the perfect first crop for a small space. They grow fast, they are used in small amounts so a few plants go a long way, and most of them are happy in a pot just 15 centimetres wide. A windowsill garden also cuts packaging: no plastic herb packets, no herbs going slimy in the back of the fridge. And there is a simple pleasure in snipping fresh mint for tea or basil for pasta.

The Easiest Herbs for Beginners

Start with the forgiving ones. These five are the most reliable on a windowsill:

  • Basil — loves warmth and sun; pinch the top leaves often and it grows bushier.
  • Mint — almost impossible to kill, but keep it in its own pot or it will take over everything.
  • Parsley — slow to start from seed, so buy a small plant; it tolerates less sun than most herbs.
  • Chives — hardy and low-maintenance; snip with scissors and they regrow quickly.
  • Rosemary — likes sun and dry soil; a single plant can last for years.

Buy small plants rather than seeds for your first try — you get usable herbs in days instead of weeks. Seeds are cheap and fun later.

Setting Up Your Windowsill Garden

Light comes first

Most herbs want four to six hours of direct sun a day, so a south- or west-facing window is ideal. An east-facing window works too, especially for parsley and mint. A north-facing window is the hardest — if that is all you have, choose shade-tolerant herbs like mint and parsley. Rotate the pots a quarter turn every few days so the plants grow straight instead of leaning toward the glass.

Pots, soil, and drainage

Use pots with drainage holes and a saucer underneath — herbs hate sitting in water. Terracotta is a good choice because it breathes and dries out between waterings. Fill with ordinary multi-purpose compost, or mix in a little perlite for drainage. Leave about two centimetres at the top so water does not spill over. One plant per pot is the rule: crowded herbs compete for light and water.

Watering and Feeding

Watering is where most beginners go wrong, and the mistake is almost always too much rather than too little. Check the soil with a finger first: if the top two centimetres are dry, water thoroughly until a little runs out of the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. If the soil is still damp, wait. In summer that might be every two or three days; in winter, once a week is often enough. Feed lightly — a weak liquid feed once a month in the growing season is plenty.

Harvesting Without Killing the Plant

Picking herbs the right way keeps them producing for months. Always snip from the top, just above a pair of leaves, and never take more than a third of the plant at once. Regular picking encourages bushy growth and stops basil from flowering and going bitter. If a plant does flower, pinch the flowers off. For leafy herbs like basil and parsley, morning is the best time to harvest. Wash gently, use straight away, or store cut stems in a glass of water in the fridge.

Common Problems and Quick Fixes

Yellow leaves usually mean overwatering — let the soil dry out and check the drainage. Leggy, pale growth means not enough light — move the pot to your sunniest window. Small flies around the soil are fungus gnats; they thrive in damp compost, so water less and let the surface dry. If a plant looks truly unhappy, do not be discouraged: even experienced growers lose plants. Replace it, adjust one thing, and try again. If you enjoy this, your next step might be growing microgreens indoors, which use the same windowsill skills and are ready to eat in about ten days — or try growing onions at home for a crop that stores for months.

Start With One Pot Today

You do not need a garden centre trip or a grand plan. One pot of basil, one sunny window, and a weekly watering habit is a complete windowsill garden. Once that plant is thriving, add mint, then parsley, and soon your cooking will have a fresh ingredient growing beside the stove.
