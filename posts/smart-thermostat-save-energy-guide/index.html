Smart Thermostats: A Simple Guide to Saving Energy at Home

Heating and cooling is usually the largest share of a home energy bill, and much of it is wasted heating empty rooms or keeping a house at full comfort temperature while everyone is out. A smart thermostat tackles exactly that waste. It learns your routine, follows a schedule you set, and can be adjusted from your phone — so the heating runs when you need it and eases off when you do not. Used well, it saves energy without you feeling cold. This guide explains how to choose one and use it well.

What a Smart Thermostat Actually Does

At its simplest, a smart thermostat is a heating control you can programme and adjust remotely. Most models go further: they learn when you are usually home and adjust the temperature automatically, and some work room by room with extra sensors, so a spare bedroom stays cooler while the living room stays warm. The saving follows the principle in our guide to saving energy at home: heat the home you use, when you use it.

Is a Smart Thermostat Worth It for Your Home?

A smart thermostat helps most in homes where the heating runs on a fixed timer that nobody adjusts, where people are out at irregular hours, or where different rooms need different temperatures. It helps least in homes already heated carefully by hand, in very small flats, or where the heating system is unusual. Before buying, check compatibility with your boiler or heat pump and your wiring. Most makers publish a compatibility checker on their website — use it, and take a photo of your existing thermostat wiring before you touch anything. If the wiring looks unfamiliar, or your system uses electric storage heaters or underfloor heating with its own controls, get advice from a qualified installer rather than guessing. A thermostat that does not suit your system will not save energy.

How to Choose One

Start with compatibility, then compare features that matter daily. A clear, easy-to-change schedule matters more than a colourful display. Look for these practical points:

  • Simple scheduling. Set different temperatures for mornings, evenings and weekends in a few taps, and override them easily.
  • Presence sensing or geofencing. The thermostat notices when the house is empty and drops to an eco temperature.
  • Room sensors or zoning. Useful in larger homes or homes where one room is always too hot and another always cold.
  • Energy reports. A monthly summary shows whether your changes are working.
  • No subscription surprises. Check whether the features you want need an ongoing payment, and what the thermostat can do without it.
  • Manual control that still works. Guests and visitors should be able to turn the heating up or down without an app or an account.

Setting It Up to Actually Save Energy

Installation is usually straightforward for a standard system, but follow the maker's instructions exactly and switch off the power at the fuse box first. If anything is unclear, use a qualified installer. Start with a simple schedule matching your real week: comfortable when you are home and awake, lower overnight, and lower again when the house is empty. Give each new setting a few days before judging it, and set the away temperature low enough to save energy but high enough to protect pipes in cold weather.

Schedules That Work in Real Life

The best schedule is one you barely notice. A common pattern is warm in the morning and evening, cooler while everyone is out and overnight. Use the holiday or away mode when you travel, rather than leaving the normal schedule running in an empty house. If your thermostat learns from your adjustments, let it: turning the heating down when you feel warm teaches it your real preferences. Review the energy report monthly — a room using more than expected may point to a draughty window. Sealing draughts is one of the cheapest wins, as our guide to draught-proofing your home explains.

Habits That Matter More Than the Device

No thermostat can fix a home that leaks heat. Check the basics: close curtains at dusk, keep doors shut in rooms you are heating, bleed radiators that are cold at the top, and do not block radiators with furniture. Keep the thermostat away from draughts, sunlight and lamps, which confuse its readings. Constantly nudging the temperature up and down wastes energy — set a schedule you trust and let it run.

The Bottom Line

A smart thermostat earns its place when it matches your heating to your real life: warm when you are home, cooler when you are not. Check compatibility first, choose simple scheduling over gimmicks, and give your settings time to settle. Combined with draught-proofing and sensible habits, it cuts heating waste while your home stays just as comfortable.
