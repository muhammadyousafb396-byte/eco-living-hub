Grow Tomatoes in Containers: A Beginner Guide to a Big Harvest

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-11-03 · Category: Urban Gardening

  • Pick a Container and the Right Soil
  • How to Plant Tomatoes in Pots

Homegrown tomatoes are warm, sweet and full of flavour, and they are surprisingly easy to grow in a container. You do not need a garden bed or years of experience. A sunny balcony or patio, a large pot, good soil and regular watering are enough for a generous harvest in a single season.

Choose the Right Tomato Variety

Container tomatoes fall into two main types. Determinate, or bush, varieties grow to a set size, fruit over a few weeks and then stop. They are compact, need little pruning and suit smaller pots. Indeterminate, or cordon, varieties keep growing and fruiting all season. They give a longer harvest but need a tall support and regular pruning. For a first container crop, look for varieties described as suitable for pots or patio growing, especially cherry and plum types. Cherry tomatoes are forgiving, fruit early and produce heavily, which makes them ideal for beginners. A variety bred for containers will cope better with a pot than a tall variety bred for a greenhouse bed.

Pick a Container and the Right Soil

Tomatoes need room for roots, so choose a container at least 30 centimetres wide and deep, with drainage holes in the bottom. A pot that is too small dries out quickly and limits the harvest. Fabric grow bags work well too: they are light, drain freely and are easy to store over winter. Fill the container with good-quality potting compost, never soil dug from the garden, which compacts in pots and drains poorly. Mix in a little compost or well-rotted manure if you have it. Place the pot where it will get at least six to eight hours of direct sun a day. A south- or west-facing balcony or patio is ideal, and a wall behind the pot gives shelter from wind and reflects extra warmth. If you are new to small-space growing, our guide to growing food on a balcony covers sunlight, containers and watering in more detail.

How to Plant Tomatoes in Pots

Start seeds indoors six to eight weeks before the last frost, or buy young plants from a garden centre once the weather has warmed. Harden plants off for a week by putting them outside for longer each day before planting them out. Plant one tomato per large pot, burying the stem a little deeper than it sat in its nursery pot. Tomatoes grow roots along a buried stem, so deeper planting gives a stronger plant. Water the pot thoroughly after planting and add a sturdy stake, cane or tomato cage straight away. Putting the support in at planting time avoids damaging the roots later. Finish with a mulch of straw or compost around the base to hold moisture in the soil.

Watering, Feeding and Support

Watering is the job that decides whether container tomatoes thrive. Pots dry out fast in sun and wind, so check the soil every day in warm weather by pressing a finger a few centimetres deep. Water deeply until it drains from the bottom, and water the soil rather than the leaves, which helps prevent disease. Water in the morning so the plant dries before evening. Irregular watering can split fruit and cause blossom end rot, where the base of the fruit turns dark. Feed every one to two weeks once flowers appear, using a liquid tomato fertiliser at the strength shown on the bottle. Tie the main stem loosely to its support as it grows, and pinch out the small side shoots where a leaf joins the stem on indeterminate varieties. For more ideas on feeding container crops, see our guide to growing vegetables in a small space.

Harvesting Your Tomatoes

Pick tomatoes when they are fully coloured, slightly soft and come away with a gentle tug. Fruit left to ripen fully on the plant tastes far better than fruit picked green and ripened indoors, so patience pays. Harvest regularly, because picking ripe fruit encourages the plant to keep producing. Eat them the same day if you can, or store ripe tomatoes at room temperature for a few days. Never store fresh tomatoes in the fridge if you can avoid it, as cold dulls their flavour. At the end of the season, pull up spent plants and add healthy stems to your compost, leaving any diseased material out of the bin.

The Bottom Line

Container tomatoes are proof that a small space can produce a serious harvest. Choose a compact or cherry variety, give it a large pot, good compost, plenty of sun and steady water, and feed it through the season. Do that, and your balcony or patio should keep you in sweet, sun-warmed tomatoes all summer long.
