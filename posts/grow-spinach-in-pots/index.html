Grow Spinach in Pots: A Beginner Guide to Fresh Homegrown Spinach

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-11-11 · Category: Urban Gardening

  • Pick a Pot With Room to Grow

Spinach is one of the easiest vegetables to grow in a pot. It grows fast, needs little space, and gives fresh leaves for salads, smoothies, and cooking for weeks. You do not need a garden bed or any experience — a pot on a balcony, patio, or bright windowsill is enough. This guide shows you how.

Choose the Right Variety for Pots

Most spinach varieties grow well in containers, but some suit pots better than others. Smooth-leaf types are the easiest choice for beginners because their flat leaves are simple to wash and quick to pick. Baby-leaf varieties are bred to be harvested young and tender, which makes them ideal for salads. If you garden in a warm climate, look for a slow-bolting or heat-tolerant variety on the seed packet, because ordinary spinach rushes to flower when the weather turns hot. Sow cool-season types in spring and autumn, and heat-tolerant types for summer. If you enjoy growing leafy greens in small spaces, our guide to growing salad leaves at home uses the same cut-and-come-again routine and pairs well with spinach.

Pick a Pot With Room to Grow

Spinach has shallow roots, so it does not need a deep container, but it does need width. Choose a pot at least 25 to 30 centimetres wide and 20 centimetres deep, with drainage holes in the bottom. A wide, shallow container, a window box, or a grow bag all work well. Fill it with good multi-purpose compost and leave about 2 centimetres of space at the top for watering. Place it where it gets four to six hours of sun a day. In very hot weather, a spot with morning sun and afternoon shade keeps the leaves tender and slows bolting. For more ideas on fitting food plants into tight spaces, see our balcony urban gardening guide.

Soil and Sowing

Spinach likes rich, moist soil that drains freely. Mix a handful of well-rotted compost or worm castings into the compost before sowing to give the young plants a good start. Sow seeds 1 to 2 centimetres deep and 5 centimetres apart, then water gently. Seeds sprout within 7 to 14 days, depending on the temperature. Thin seedlings to about 8 to 10 centimetres apart once they are a few centimetres tall. For a continuous supply, sow a small new batch every two to three weeks rather than sowing everything at once. That way you always have young plants coming along as the older ones finish.

Watering and Feeding

Water is the most important part of growing spinach. The soil should stay evenly moist at all times, because dry soil makes the leaves tough and pushes the plant to bolt. In warm weather, check the pot every day and water at the base of the plants in the morning, keeping the leaves as dry as you can. A thin mulch of straw or dried leaves slows evaporation and keeps the roots cool. Feed lightly with liquid seaweed or balanced organic fertiliser every three to four weeks — heavy feeding makes soft leaves that spoil quickly.

Harvesting Cut-and-Come-Again

Spinach is ready to pick 40 to 50 days after sowing, or sooner if you like baby leaves. Pick the outer leaves first, when they are 8 to 10 centimetres long, and leave the centre of the plant to keep growing. This cut-and-come-again method gives you harvest after harvest from the same plant for several weeks. Snip leaves off cleanly rather than tearing them. Harvest in the morning when the leaves are crisp and full of moisture, and pick little and often — regular picking encourages the plant to produce more leaves instead of running to seed. If you grow other quick crops, our guide to growing microgreens indoors is a good match for the same kitchen windowsill.

Common Problems and Easy Fixes

Bolting is the most common problem: the plant suddenly sends up a tall flower stalk and the leaves turn bitter. It is triggered by heat and dry soil, so water well, mulch, and move the pot into partial shade on the hottest days. Once a plant bolts, pull it up and sow a fresh batch. Slugs love young spinach, so check under the pot rim at night and pick them off by hand, or set a simple beer trap nearby. Yellowing leaves usually mean the plant is hungry or the pot is waterlogged, so feed lightly and check that the drainage holes are clear. Pale, thin seedlings that stretch toward the light need more sun — move the pot to a brighter spot. With a wide pot, steady water, and regular picking, growing spinach in pots is one of the quickest wins in the kitchen garden, and the taste of leaves picked minutes before a meal is hard to beat.
