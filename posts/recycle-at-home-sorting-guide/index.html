How to Recycle at Home: A Simple Sorting Guide That Actually Works

Recycling at home sounds simple, but the rules can feel confusing. Some packaging looks recyclable and is not. Here is the short answer: sort your waste by material, keep recyclables clean and dry, and check your local rules for the few items that vary. The guide below turns that into a routine you can follow without thinking about it every time you stand at the bin.

Why Sorting Matters

Recycling only works when materials arrive at the sorting facility in usable condition. Paper soaked in food waste, plastic coated in grease, or glass mixed with general rubbish can all end up rejected. When a load is too contaminated, the whole batch may be sent to landfill instead of being recycled. Sorting at home is the step that decides whether your effort counts.

What Goes in Each Bin

Paper and cardboard

Newspapers, magazines, office paper, envelopes, cereal boxes, and flattened cardboard boxes all belong in the paper bin. Flatten boxes to save space and keep paper dry. Greasy pizza boxes, used tissues, and paper towels do not belong here.

Plastic

Most household recycling takes rigid plastics such as bottles, tubs, trays, and pots. Empty them, give them a quick rinse if they held food, and put lids back on bottles where your local rules allow it. Soft plastics such as carrier bags, cling film, and crisp packets usually need a separate store drop-off, not your home bin. When in doubt, check the label and your local guidance rather than guessing.

Glass

Glass bottles and jars are widely recycled and can be reused endlessly without losing quality. Rinse out food and drink, remove metal lids, and place them in the glass bin. Broken glass should be wrapped carefully and handled according to local rules, and items such as drinking glasses, mirrors, and light bulbs do not belong in the glass bin because they are made from different types of glass.

Metal

Drink cans, food tins, foil trays, and clean aluminium foil are all recyclable. Rinse tins, squash cans to save space, and scrunch foil into a ball roughly the size of a tennis ball so it does not slip through sorting machinery. Aerosol cans that are completely empty can usually go in too, but never put in anything that still contains paint, gas, or hazardous chemicals.

The Rinse-and-Dry Rule

You do not need to wash recyclables like dishes. A quick rinse under the tap and a shake dry is enough for jars, tins, and tubs. The goal is to remove food residue that could attract pests or spoil paper in the same load. Let items dry before they go in the bin, especially if paper and containers share a collection.

Common Recycling Mistakes to Avoid

  • Wishcycling: putting something in the recycling bin and hoping it is recyclable. If you are not sure, check local rules or put it in general waste. One wrong item can contaminate a load.
  • Bagging recyclables: loose items sort far better than items tied inside a plastic bag. Keep recyclables loose in the bin unless your collector says otherwise.
  • Leaving food in containers: half-full jars and unrinsed takeaway boxes spoil paper and cardboard around them. Empty and rinse first.
  • Recycling coffee cups: most takeaway coffee cups have a plastic lining and need specialist recycling. Reusable cups are the simpler answer.

What Recycling Cannot Take

Some everyday items simply do not belong in home recycling: nappies, wet wipes, polystyrene packaging, chip bags, garden hoses, textiles, and electronics. Many of these have separate routes. Textiles can go to charity shops or textile banks, electronics to e-waste collections, and batteries to store drop-off points.

Set Up a Simple Home Sorting Station

A sorting station does not need to be fancy. Three labelled bins or bags, one for paper and cardboard, one for plastic and metal, and one for glass, cover most homes. Put them where waste is created, usually the kitchen, and add a small caddy for food scraps if you compost. Reducing waste at the source makes sorting easier still: zero-waste kitchen swaps cut the packaging coming into your home, and composting at home takes food scraps out of the bin entirely.

The Bottom Line

Good recycling is a habit, not a test. Sort by material, keep things clean and dry, and learn the handful of local exceptions that apply where you live. Once the station is set up and the routine is clear, recycling takes no more time than throwing things away, and far more of what you use gets a second life.
