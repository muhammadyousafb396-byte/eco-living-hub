Grow Onions at Home: A Simple Beginner Guide

Onions are one of the most useful vegetables you can grow at home. They cost very little to plant, they need almost no special care, and a single row can keep your kitchen supplied for months. If you have a sunny spot — a garden bed, a raised bed, or even a deep pot on a balcony — you can grow onions. This guide covers the simplest method: growing from onion sets, which are small, ready-to-plant bulbs that give beginners the best chance of success.

Why Grow Your Own Onions

A bag of onion sets costs about the same as two or three shop-bought onions, yet it can grow dozens of bulbs. Homegrown onions also taste sweeter and fresher than many shop varieties, and you know exactly how they were grown — no sprays you did not choose. Onions store well, too: kept cool and dry, a good harvest lasts for months, so the work you do in spring pays off all year.

Onion Sets or Seeds?

You can grow onions from seed or from sets. For beginners, sets are the better choice.

Onion Sets

Sets are small, immature bulbs sold in bags or nets. They are ready to plant straight into the soil, they grow fast, and they are far less likely to fail than seeds. Most garden centres sell them in spring, and a bag of 50 to 100 sets is enough for a family.

Onion Seeds

Seeds are cheaper and offer more varieties, but they need to be started indoors in late winter and need more care. Save seeds for your second year, once you know the basics.

When and Where to Plant

Plant onion sets in early to mid-spring, as soon as the soil is workable and not waterlogged. Onions need sun — at least six hours a day — and soil that drains well, because bulbs rot in standing water. If your soil is heavy clay, mix in compost before planting, or grow in a raised bed or a deep container. If you enjoy growing bulbs, our guide to growing garlic at home uses almost the same method, so the two crops go well together.

How to Plant Onion Sets

  • Push each set into the soil, pointed end up, so just the tip shows above the surface.
  • Space the sets about 10 cm apart in rows 25 to 30 cm apart — crowded onions stay small.
  • Water them in gently, then keep the bed weed-free while the bulbs settle in.
  • If birds pull the sets out, cover the bed with netting for the first two weeks.

Watering and Feeding

Onions need steady moisture while the bulbs are swelling, especially in dry weather, but they hate sitting in wet soil. Water deeply once or twice a week rather than a little every day, and stop watering once the leaves start to flop over and yellow — that is the plant telling you the bulbs are ready. Our homemade plant fertilizer guide has a simple recipe if you would rather feed with something you made yourself.

Common Problems

Bolting

Sometimes an onion sends up a flower stalk. This is called bolting, and it usually happens after a cold snap. Pull bolted onions first and use them straight away — they will not store well.

Pests and Disease

Onion fly is the main pest. A fine mesh cover over the bed from planting time keeps the flies away without chemicals. Good spacing and dry soil also prevent most rot.

When and How to Harvest

Onions are ready when the leaves turn yellow and fall over, usually in mid to late summer. Choose a dry day, loosen the soil with a fork, and pull the bulbs gently. Leave them on the soil surface or on a rack in the sun for a week or two to dry and cure — this toughens the skins and makes them store for months. If rain is forecast, move them under cover.

Storing Your Onions

Once the skins feel papery and the necks are dry, trim off the roots and store the bulbs in a cool, dry, dark place — a net bag or a basket works well. A well-cured onion keeps for three to six months, so your spring planting can flavour meals right through the winter.

Start With Onions, Grow From There

Growing onions is one of the easiest ways to start growing your own food. A sunny spot, a bag of sets, and a little patience are all it takes. Start small this year, and once you taste your first homegrown onion, you may find yourself planning a bigger vegetable patch next spring.
