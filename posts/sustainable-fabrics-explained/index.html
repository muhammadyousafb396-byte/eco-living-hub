Sustainable Fabrics Explained: Cotton, Linen, Hemp and More

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-10-29 · Category: Sustainable Fashion

On this page

  • Cotton: Familiar, Breathable, and Versatile
  • Linen and Hemp: Strong Plant Fibres
  • Wool, Silk, and Animal Fibres
  • Synthetics and Blends: Practical but Tricky
  • How to Choose Fabric for Real Life

Fabric labels use a lot of words that sound similar, and it can be hard to know what they mean for comfort, durability, and care. The fabric you choose affects how a garment feels, how it washes, how long it lasts, and how easy it is to repair. No single fabric is right for every item. A summer shirt, a winter jumper, and a pair of work trousers all have different needs. This guide explains the most common fabrics in plain language, so you can read a label, compare options, and choose the fabric that suits the job.

Cotton: Familiar, Breathable, and Versatile

Cotton is soft, breathable, and easy to care for, which is why it appears in shirts, T-shirts, jeans, towels, and bedding. It absorbs moisture, feels comfortable against skin, and usually washes well. Organic cotton is grown without synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, following farming standards that vary by certification. Recycled cotton is made from offcuts or old garments and can reduce the need for new fibre. Both can be good choices, but the quality of the finished fabric matters too. A tightly woven cotton shirt will usually hold its shape better than a very thin one. Check the weave, the seams, and how the fabric feels in your hands. If it feels flimsy before you buy it, it is unlikely to improve with washing.

Caring for Cotton

Most cotton can be washed in cold or warm water and air-dried. Washing less often and avoiding high heat helps cotton keep its colour and shape. Our eco-friendly laundry routine explains cold washing and air-drying in more detail, and those habits suit cotton particularly well.

Linen and Hemp: Strong Plant Fibres

Linen is made from flax plants. It is strong, breathable, and naturally textured, with a relaxed look that suits warm weather. Linen can feel crisp at first and softens with washing. It dries quickly and often needs less washing than cotton, because it airs out well. Hemp is another strong plant fibre. It makes sturdy fabric for shirts, trousers, and bags, and it blends well with cotton or linen to add softness. Both linen and hemp tend to wrinkle, which is part of their natural look. If you prefer a smooth finish, a light press with an iron while the fabric is slightly damp usually does the job. When shopping for linen or hemp, look for a firm weave and neat finishing, and check that buttons and seams are secure.

Wool, Silk, and Animal Fibres

Wool is warm, naturally breathable, and resists odours, so woollen jumpers and coats often need less washing than other clothes. Merino wool is soft and fine, while thicker wools suit coats and blankets. Wool needs gentle care. Wash by hand or on a wool cycle in cold water, and dry flat to avoid shrinking or stretching. Moths can damage stored wool, so keep woollen clothes clean and store them with cedar or lavender if you use them. Silk is smooth, light, and strong for its weight, but it needs delicate washing and protection from strong sun. Both wool and silk can last a long time when cared for gently, and both can be repaired, darned, or rewoven by a specialist when needed.

Synthetics and Blends: Practical but Tricky

  • Choose synthetics where their strengths matter, such as rainwear and sport.
  • Choose natural fibres where breathability and comfort matter most.
  • Check blends: a small amount of stretch can help fit, a large amount can change how fabric ages.
  • Wash synthetics in a laundry bag to catch loose fibres when you can.
  • Air-dry synthetics away from high heat to protect shape and finish.

How to Choose Fabric for Real Life

For winter layers, wool offers warmth without bulk. For active days, a synthetic or blend that dries quickly may be the practical choice. For trousers and jackets that take daily wear, look for a firm weave and strong seams, whatever the fibre. Then think about care. If you know you will not hand-wash or iron often, choose fabrics that fit your routine. A garment that needs care you will not give is unlikely to last, however good its fabric claims sound.

Finally, look at the whole garment. Fabric is only one part of quality. Good stitching, secure buttons, a lining that does not pull, and a cut that fits well all matter just as much. When you build a wardrobe around fabrics you understand and clothes you enjoy wearing, shopping becomes simpler.
