How to Start Composting at Home (Even in an Apartment)

By Eco Living Hub · Published 2026-10-26 · Category: Eco Home

On this page

  • Pick the Right Method for Your Space
  • What to Compost (and What to Skip)
  • Troubleshooting Common Problems

Nearly a third of household trash is food scraps and yard waste, and most of it could become compost instead. Composting sounds like something that requires a big backyard, but it works just as well in a small apartment. All you need is a container, the right mix of materials, and a little patience. Within a few months, your banana peels and coffee grounds become dark, rich compost that your plants will love.

What Composting Actually Does

Composting is nature's recycling system. Microorganisms break down organic matter into a soil-like material called humus. When food scraps go to a landfill, they rot without oxygen and release methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. In a compost bin, the same scraps break down with oxygen and produce something useful instead. You reduce your trash, cut methane emissions, and get free fertilizer. It is one of the simplest climate-friendly habits a household can adopt.

Pick the Right Method for Your Space

1. Countertop composting with a small bin

If you only want to collect scraps for a community garden or a local drop-off site, a small countertop bin is enough. Keep a lidded container with a charcoal filter near your sink, empty it every few days, and you will never deal with smells. Many cities now offer curbside compost pickup or drop-off points at farmers markets.

2. Bokashi composting

Bokashi is ideal for apartments because it works in a sealed bucket and handles foods other methods cannot, including meat, dairy, and cooked leftovers. You layer scraps with bokashi bran, press out the air, and let the mixture ferment for about two weeks. The result is then buried in soil or added to a traditional compost pile to finish breaking down. The bucket is small, odor-free when sealed, and fits under a sink.

3. Worm composting (vermicomposting)

A worm bin uses red wiggler worms to eat your scraps and produce worm castings, one of the best natural fertilizers available. A bin the size of a storage tote fits in a closet or under a table. The worms eat fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and shredded paper, and they work quietly with no smell when the bin is balanced. Harvesting castings every few months gives you a powerful boost for houseplants and balcony gardens.

4. Tumbler composting for balconies

If you have a balcony or small patio, a compact tumbler is a good option. You add scraps and dry materials, spin the drum a few times a week, and the tumbling speeds up decomposition. Tumblers keep pests out, look tidy, and produce finished compost in as little as six to eight weeks in warm weather.

What to Compost (and What to Skip)

Compost needs a balance of greens and browns. Greens are wet, nitrogen-rich materials like fruit peels, vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, and fresh grass. Browns are dry, carbon-rich materials like shredded newspaper, cardboard, dry leaves, and straw. Aim for roughly three parts browns to one part greens by volume. This balance keeps the pile from getting soggy or smelly.

  • Yes: fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds and filters, eggshells, tea bags, shredded paper, dry leaves, small yard trimmings.
  • No: meat, dairy, and oily foods in open piles (bokashi is the exception), diseased plants, treated wood, glossy or coated paper, and pet waste.

Troubleshooting Common Problems

Most compost problems have simple fixes. If your bin smells, it usually needs more browns and better airflow, so add shredded cardboard and stir it. If nothing seems to be breaking down, the pile may be too dry, so sprinkle in some water until it feels like a wrung-out sponge. If fruit flies appear, bury fresh scraps under a layer of browns instead of leaving them on top. Composting is forgiving, and a small adjustment usually solves the issue within days.

The Bottom Line

You do not need land, special skills, or expensive equipment to compost. Pick one method that fits your home, start with your daily kitchen scraps, and let nature do the rest. In a few months you will throw away less trash and have free, nutrient-rich compost for every plant you own.
