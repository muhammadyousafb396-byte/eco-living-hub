Grow Lemons in Pots: A Beginner Guide to Fresh Homegrown Lemons

There is nothing quite like picking a fresh lemon from a tree you grew yourself. Lemons are one of the easiest fruit trees to grow in a pot, and a single tree can give you fruit for most of the year. You do not need a garden. A sunny patio or balcony is enough for a dwarf lemon tree in a large pot.

Why Lemons Grow Well in Pots

Lemon trees suit containers because you control the soil, the watering, and the position. In the ground, a lemon tree can grow very large. In a pot, it stays between one and two metres tall, and you can move it into shelter when cold weather arrives. Pots also warm up faster in spring, which helps young trees grow quickly. It pairs well with other container crops — see our guide to growing tomatoes in containers.

Pick the Right Lemon Variety

Not every lemon tree suits a pot, so start with a variety bred for containers. Meyer lemons are the most popular choice for beginners. They are a cross between a lemon and a mandarin, so the fruit is slightly sweeter and less sharp than a shop-bought lemon, and the tree is naturally compact and very productive. Lisbon and Eureka are the classic sharp lemons and also grow well as dwarf trees. Always buy a grafted tree from a nursery rather than growing from a seed. A seed-grown tree can take years to fruit and may taste nothing like its parent, while a grafted tree usually fruits within one to two years.

Choosing the Pot and Soil

Pick the biggest pot you can manage, at least 40 litres and about 45 centimetres wide and deep, with plenty of drainage holes. Lemons hate sitting in water, so drainage matters more than almost anything else. Terracotta pots breathe and let soil dry between waterings, but they are heavy. Plastic pots are lighter and hold moisture longer. Either works as long as water can escape freely, so raise the pot on feet or bricks and never let it sit in a puddle. Fill the pot with a free-draining citrus compost, or a mix of multi-purpose compost and coarse grit. Ordinary garden soil is too heavy for pots and compacts around the roots, so do not use it.

Planting and Daily Care

Plant your lemon tree at the same depth it was growing in its nursery pot, firm the compost gently around the roots, and water well. Place the pot in the sunniest spot you have. Lemons need at least six hours of direct sun a day to fruit well, and more is better. South-facing patios and balconies are ideal, and indoors a bright south-facing window works, though fruiting may be lighter.

Watering is the skill that matters most. Lemon trees like steady moisture but not soggy soil. In warm weather, check the soil every couple of days by pressing a finger an inch deep. If it feels dry, water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom. In winter, water far less, only when the top few centimetres feel dry. Feed your tree from spring to early autumn with a citrus fertiliser, or use a homemade feed such as our homemade plant fertiliser made from kitchen scraps, which suits container plants well. Prune lightly once a year in late winter: remove dead or crossing branches and thin the centre so light reaches inside. Do not prune hard, as lemons fruit on new growth.

Protecting Your Tree in Winter

Lemon trees are not frost-hardy. A light frost can damage leaves, and a hard frost can kill a young tree. If you live somewhere with cold winters, move the pot into a bright, frost-free spot before the first frost, such as a conservatory, a sunny porch, or a cool room indoors. Water sparingly while it rests. If you cannot move it, wrap the pot in bubble wrap and cover the tree with horticultural fleece on frosty nights.

Harvesting Your Lemons

Lemons can take six to nine months to ripen after flowering, so be patient. Pick them when they are fully yellow, slightly soft when squeezed, and easy to twist off the branch. Do not pull hard, or you may damage the branch. One healthy potted tree can produce dozens of lemons a year once it is established. Use them in cooking, baking, and drinks, or slice and freeze them for later.

The Bottom Line

Growing lemons in pots is one of the most rewarding things a small-space gardener can do. Choose a dwarf variety like Meyer, give it a big pot, a sunny spot, and steady watering, and protect it from frost. Within a year or two, you will be picking your own lemons, and your patio will smell wonderful every time the tree flowers.
