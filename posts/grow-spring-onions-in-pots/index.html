Grow Spring Onions in Pots: A Beginner Guide to Fresh Onions All Year

Spring onions are the perfect first crop for a new gardener. They grow fast, need very little space, and you can harvest them again and again from the same pot. A few pots on a sunny windowsill or balcony can keep you in fresh green onions for salads, stir-fries and garnishes all year round — and they cost almost nothing to start.

Why Spring Onions Are Ideal for Pots

Spring onions, also called scallions, are slim, mild onions harvested while they are still young and green. Because they are picked before they form a bulb, they do not need deep soil or a long growing season. A pot just 15 centimetres deep is enough, and you can sow them from early spring right through to autumn. They also grow well indoors, so a bright windowsill works in winter too. If you already grow salad leaves at home, spring onions are a natural next crop — they take the same kind of care and the same sunny spot.

Two Ways to Start: Seeds or Shop-Bought Stems

You can grow spring onions from seed or by regrowing stems from a bunch you bought at the shop. Seeds are cheaper and give you more plants: sow them thinly, about 1 centimetre deep, and seedlings appear in about two weeks. Regrowing is quicker and almost free. Cut off the green tops for cooking, keep the white root ends about 5 centimetres long, and stand them in a glass of water on a windowsill. New green shoots appear within days, and after a week you can plant them in compost.

Choosing Pots and Soil

Almost any container works as long as it has drainage holes: terracotta pots, plastic tubs, window boxes or a large tin with holes punched in the bottom. A pot 20 to 30 centimetres wide holds enough plants for a household, and a window box is ideal because you can sow in neat rows. Fill the pot with multi-purpose compost mixed with a little grit or perlite so water drains freely — spring onions hate sitting in wet soil.

Sowing and Planting, Step by Step

Water the compost until it is damp but not soggy. Scatter seeds thinly across the surface or sow in shallow rows, cover with a thin layer of compost, and water gently with a fine rose or spray bottle. Place the pot in the sunniest spot you have — at least six hours of sunlight a day is ideal — and keep the compost damp until seedlings appear. When seedlings are about 5 centimetres tall, thin them so plants stand 2 to 3 centimetres apart, and eat the thinnings in a salad. For regrown stems, plant them 3 centimetres apart with the roots in the compost and the shoots above the surface, then water well.

Watering and Feeding

Spring onions need steady moisture but not constant wetness. Water when the top couple of centimetres of compost feel dry, and water in the morning so the leaves dry during the day. In hot weather a pot can dry out in a day, so check daily in summer. They are light feeders, so one weak liquid feed every two to three weeks is plenty; too much fertiliser makes soft, floppy growth. If you want to save water while you garden, our water-saving garden habits show how to water pots without waste.

Harvesting Again and Again

You can start harvesting when the onions are about as thick as a pencil, usually eight to ten weeks after sowing. Pull the whole plant, or cut the green tops about 3 centimetres above the soil and let them regrow — the second method gives you two or three harvests from one plant. Sow a fresh batch every three to four weeks from spring to early autumn for a continuous supply.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is sowing seeds too thickly and never thinning them — crowded plants stay thin and weak. Another is letting pots dry out completely, which makes the onions tough and slow to grow; a saucer under the pot helps in summer. Finally, do not wait too long to harvest: spring onions left in the ground too long form a fat bulb and lose their mild flavour.

Fresh Onions, All Year Round

Growing spring onions in pots is one of the easiest wins in the garden: cheap seed, a small pot, a sunny spot, and fresh onions in a couple of months. For more easy pot crops, try our guides to growing radishes in pots and growing spinach in pots.
