Grow Sweet Potatoes in Containers: A Beginner Guide to a Sweet Homegrown Harvest

Sweet potatoes are one of the most rewarding vegetables you can grow in a pot. A single plant can produce several kilos of sweet, orange-fleshed roots, and the vines spill over the edge of the container in a cascade of heart-shaped leaves that look good all summer. They love heat, so they do well on a sunny patio or balcony, and they are a forgiving first crop if you have never grown your own food.

Why Sweet Potatoes Suit Containers

Sweet potatoes are not related to ordinary potatoes at all. They are a tropical plant that needs warm soil and a long, sunny season, which makes containers ideal — pot soil warms up faster, and you can move the pot to the sunniest spot you have. Containers also keep the roots contained, so harvesting means tipping the pot out instead of digging through a bed. They grow well alongside other container crops — see our guide to growing potatoes in containers.

Start With Slips, Not Seeds

Sweet potatoes are grown from slips, which are small shoots sprouted from a mature sweet potato. You can buy slips from a nursery in late spring, or grow your own by resting a sweet potato half-submerged in a jar of water, held up with toothpicks. Within two to three weeks shoots appear on top and roots grow below. When the shoots reach about 15 centimetres, twist them off and stand them in water until they grow roots. Each slip becomes one plant, so three or four are plenty for a large container. Plant out only when the soil is properly warm — sweet potatoes sulk in cold soil and will not recover from a late frost.

Choose the Right Container and Soil

Pick the biggest container you can manage, at least 40 litres — sweet potatoes need room to swell underground. A deep grow bag works well and is easy to empty at harvest. Fill it with a loose, free-draining mix of multi-purpose compost and a handful of grit or perlite; heavy, compacted soil gives thin, stringy roots, so avoid garden soil on its own. They do not need rich feeding to start — too much nitrogen makes lots of leaf and very little root. A scoop of slow-release organic fertiliser at planting is enough.

Planting Your Slips

Plant each slip about 30 centimetres apart, burying the stem up to the first leaves. Water well and keep the soil moist for the first two weeks. The vines will start to spread quickly in warm weather, and a single plant can trail for two metres or more, so give it space or train the vines up a trellis, and pinch out the growing tips once or twice to push energy into the roots. A large container on a sunny balcony is still enough — see our guide to growing vegetables in a small space for more ideas.

Watering and Feeding Through Summer

Sweet potatoes need steady watering, especially in the first month and when the roots swell in late summer. Keep the soil moist but never waterlogged — a soggy pot rots the roots. Water deeply a few times a week rather than a little every day, and mulch with straw or shredded leaves to hold in moisture. Feed every three to four weeks with liquid tomato feed or comfrey tea once the vines are growing strongly, and stop about a month before harvest so the roots firm up and store better.

Harvesting and Curing Your Sweet Potatoes

Most varieties are ready 100 to 120 days after planting, usually in early autumn, when the leaves start to yellow. Dig carefully with your hands or tip the whole container onto a sheet — the roots bruise easily, and bruised roots do not store well. Let them dry in the sun for a few hours, then cure them for a week or two in a warm, humid spot, such as a sunny windowsill, which sweetens the flesh and toughens the skin. After curing, store them in a cool, dark place in a paper bag or open box. Stored properly, homegrown sweet potatoes keep for months.

Common Problems and Quick Fixes

Sweet potatoes are generally trouble-free, but a few things can go wrong. If the vines grow fast but no roots form, the soil is probably too rich in nitrogen — cut back on feeding and be patient. Slugs love young leaves, so check under the vines after rain and pick them off by hand. Whitefly can appear in hot weather; a strong spray of water knocks them off. If the roots are small, the season may have been too short or the pot too small — try a larger container and an earlier start next year.
