Natural Cleaning for Every Surface: Wood, Glass, Steel and Stone

Natural cleaning sounds simple until you reach for the same spray on every surface. Vinegar that shines glass can dull a stone countertop, and water that cleans tile can damage untreated wood. Natural cleaning is easy once you match the cleaner to the surface. Start with the surface you clean most often, and test any new mix on a small hidden spot first.

The Golden Rules Before You Start

Three rules keep natural cleaning safe. First, label every bottle with its contents and date. Second, never mix vinegar with bleach, and never mix vinegar with hydrogen peroxide in the same bottle. Third, use soft cloths: abrasive scourers scratch wood, steel and stone. For the basic recipes behind this guide, see our DIY natural cleaning products guide.

Wood: Gentle, Dry and Sparing With Water

Wood hates standing water and harsh chemicals. For sealed wooden furniture and floors, dust first, then wipe with a cloth wrung out in warm water with a few drops of mild soap, such as castile soap. Dry the surface straight away. Never soak wood, and never use vinegar as a routine wood cleaner, as the acid can dull finishes. For sticky marks, hold a warm damp cloth over the spot for a minute, then wipe gently. Keep oil away from floors, where it makes the surface slippery.

Glass and Mirrors: Less Liquid, More Buffing

Glass shows every streak, and the usual cause is too much cleaner. Mix two cups of water with half a cup of white vinegar and a quarter cup of rubbing alcohol in a spray bottle. Spray lightly, just two or three mists, and buff with a clean microfibre cloth or scrunched newspaper. Wipe in one direction, then finish with a dry cloth in the opposite direction to catch the last streaks. Clean glass out of direct sunlight, because sun dries the spray before you can buff it. For bathroom mirrors, the same mix cuts toothpaste splashes and water marks without the heavy fragrance of commercial sprays.

Stainless Steel: Wipe With the Grain

Stainless steel looks easy to clean and is easy to streak. Look closely and you will see fine lines running in one direction, called the grain. Always wipe with the grain, never in circles. Start with warm water and a drop of washing-up liquid on a soft cloth, then rinse and dry thoroughly, because water left to dry leaves spots. For fingerprints and smudges, a cloth dampened with a little white vinegar works well, followed by a dry buff. For stubborn marks, make a paste of baking soda and water, rub gently with the grain, rinse and dry. Avoid steel wool and harsh scourers, which scratch the surface permanently and leave marks that collect dirt. A dry microfibre buff at the end makes all the difference.

Natural Stone: No Vinegar, No Acid

Marble, granite, limestone and other natural stone surfaces need special care, because acids etch and dull them. Vinegar, lemon juice and many bathroom cleaners are all too acidic for stone, even when they seem gentle. Clean stone with warm water and a few drops of mild soap instead, wiped with a soft cloth and dried straight away. Mop stone floors with the same mild mix, wringing the mop well so the floor does not stay wet. Wipe spills quickly, especially wine, coffee, oil and tomato sauce, which can stain if left. When in doubt, use the mildest cleaner that works and rinse it off fully.

Tile, Grout and Sinks: Where Baking Soda Shines

Tile itself is tough, but grout is porous and holds dirt, soap scum and mould. For everyday tile cleaning, warm water with a little soap is enough. For grout, make a paste of baking soda and water, spread it along the grout lines, leave it for ten minutes, scrub with an old toothbrush and rinse. For stubborn soap scum in a shower, spray the baking soda paste lightly with vinegar and let it fizz before scrubbing, but keep vinegar away from any natural stone in the same room. Porcelain and ceramic sinks take the same treatment well. In the kitchen, a baking soda paste lifts tea and coffee stains from a sink without scratching it. This approach pairs well with a plastic-free routine for the rest of the room, such as our eco-friendly kitchen cleaning guide.

The Bottom Line

Natural cleaning works best when the cleaner suits the surface. Use mild soap and water for wood and stone, a light vinegar mix for glass, and baking soda paste for tile and grout, and always dry and buff to finish. Match the method to the material, test first on a hidden spot, and your home will stay clean without harsh sprays or damaged surfaces.
